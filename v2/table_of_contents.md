## b.	Designing good prompts - Amy
A good design prompt is open enough to spark new ideas but concrete enough that a child knows what to start building within the first minute. Start from situations children already know, such as homework, recess, bedtime or toys. Ask children to invent or create something, not to give their opinions, because making things together is the heart of co-design. Use plain, everyday words instead of jargon like "interface" or "user experience," but don't talk down to them.
## c.	Expectations of children - Amy
### i.	They only need to know how to be children
Children don't need any training to be good co-designers. Their everyday experience of being a kid is the expertise we need in the co-design session. They do not need to be able to draw or build well, or come up with ideas that are realistic and affordable. They only need to know how to be children. 

From children, we can expect crazy and impossible ideas. However, these ideas can often help uncover a real need underneath. We can also expect energy that rises and falls. Also, children's personalities might differ, so there are different ways of contributing, and all of them are important. Some children will talk a lot and give a lot of ideas, while others will build out their ideas quietly. All of these are important and count as data.

If some children are extra quiet and do not want to participate, we can guide them slowly by asking follow-up questions that help them talk a little more. It's important to let the children know that there are no wrong answers and that all their ideas are welcome.
